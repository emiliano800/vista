# Vista — Founder Thesis

The four questions every serious investor asks, answered as stories rather than
bullet points: what is your unique insight, why would you win, how do you get to
$1B, and where does this go in a few years. [VISION.md](VISION.md) has the
longer-range picture; [BUSINESS_COURSE_OF_ACTION.md](BUSINESS_COURSE_OF_ACTION.md)

Written 2026-09-24.

## 1. The unique insight

### Two rooms

There are two rooms that matter in this business, and almost nobody has stood in
both.

The first room is the back office of a twelve-person plumbing company that a
private equity firm just bought. The office manager has run the books for
nineteen years. She knows that the QuickBooks "Other" account is where the
warranty work goes, that the Tuesday invoice run has to happen before the bank
sweep, and that the new owners keep sending her spreadsheets she does not have
time to fill in. She has heard about AI. She has been pitched three tools. She
did not sign up for any of them, because none of them could tell her what would
happen to her Tuesday.

The second room is the operating partner's office at the firm that bought her
company. He has eleven portfolio companies and is closing two more this quarter.
Every one of them has a different accounting system, a different way of
reporting, and one person like her who holds the process together. His analysts
spend the first week of every month chasing exports. His investment memo promised
the LPs three points of margin from "operational integration," and he needs to
show it happening.

Vista's founding insight is that **these two people have the same problem and
neither of them can solve it alone.** She cannot adopt AI because nobody will do
the work of fitting it to her business. He cannot integrate his portfolio because
every company is a snowflake and his team is stretched across eleven of them.

We have been in both rooms. We understand why non-technical businesses hesitate,
which is not fear of AI but fear of being handed a tool and left alone with it.
And we understand what a PE firm is actually buying when it says "integration,"
which is not software but certainty that the work will get done.

### The insight, stated plainly

Everyone building workflow automation is fighting for the same customers: technical
teams who can configure a tool. The tens of millions of businesses that cannot
configure anything are unreachable to them, and they know it. Their pipelines are
full of demos to companies that will never finish onboarding.

Everyone building for private equity is building reporting. Dashboards, data
rooms, portfolio-monitoring tools. They collect numbers after the work happens.
None of them do the work.

Vista sits at the intersection and is the only company that can. **The PE firm is
the customer-acquisition channel that pure automation companies could never
build, and the automation is the product that pure PE tooling could never
deliver.** Each half is a known-hard problem on its own. Together they solve each
other's hardest part.

## 2. Why we win

### Against pure workflow-automation companies

They have better-known brands and, today, more polished tooling. They lose to us
in this market for three reasons.

**They cannot get in the door.** A workflow tool needs a champion inside the
business who will set it up. A rollup portfolio company does not have that person.
Vista does not need one. We arrive with the acquisition and the mandate.

**They do not own the outcome.** They ship software; the customer is responsible
for making it work. Vista is accountable for the integration going live and the
savings being real. In a market where the buyer is a PE firm with an investment
thesis to prove, accountability is the product.

**They learn from one side.** Their feedback loop is support tickets from
technical users. Ours is the office manager's corrections plus the operating
partner's priorities, on every deployment, from both ends of the value chain. We
learn what the work actually is and what it is actually worth, at the same time.

### Against PE reporting and portfolio-monitoring tools

They are a feature of what we do. The analyst and CFO views in Vista exist because
the service produces them as a byproduct of connecting systems and running
workflows. A reporting tool has to ask the portfolio company for numbers. Vista
already has them, because Vista is doing the work that generates them.

### Against the firms doing this by hand

Consulting firms and operating partners' internal teams do integration work today.
They win the first deployment against us on relationships. They lose the tenth on
cost, because their tenth deployment costs the same as their first and ours does
not. Every Vista deployment leaves a reusable blueprint. Theirs leaves a slide deck.

### The durable advantage

The moat is not any single agent or connector. It is the **proprietary map of how
small-business back-office work actually gets done**, built from recorded
workflows, system mappings, exception rules, and human corrections across every
company we have integrated. By the hundredth company in a category, we deploy from
a near-complete blueprint. Nobody else has that map, because nobody else was in
the room when it was drawn.

## 3. How this gets to $1B

The path is a sequence of three businesses, each one funding and de-risking the
next. The numbers below are directional; they show the shape of the math, not a
forecast.

### Business one: integration-as-a-service for rollups

Lower-middle-market PE firms close several thousand add-on acquisitions a year in
the United States alone. Each one is an integration project worth a six-figure
engagement, because the alternative is months of an operating partner's time and a
value-creation thesis that slips.

A firm doing eight add-ons a year at a low-six-figure engagement each is a
seven-figure account. A few dozen such firms is a real services business. Services
revenue is how we fund the platform and, more importantly, how we buy the data
that becomes the moat.

Shape: tens of millions in revenue, mostly services, margin improving every quarter
as the workflow library grows.

### Business two: the operating platform every portco runs on

Once a company is integrated, it keeps running on Vista. Agents keep working,
exceptions keep flowing to people, the CFO keeps closing the month in an
afternoon. That is recurring revenue per company, priced against the measured
savings, and it stays through the hold period and past the exit.

A fund with twenty portfolio companies, each paying a fraction of the SG&A that
Vista removed, is a seven-figure recurring account with services on top for every
new acquisition. Several hundred lower-middle-market firms fit that profile.

Shape: low hundreds of millions in recurring revenue, services now a minority of
the mix, deployments handled by a small FDE team working from blueprints.

### Business three: the back office for every hesitant business

The rollups exit. The companies keep running on Vista under new owners. The people
who ran them start new businesses and call us. The accountants and brokers who
serviced them start recommending us. By this point Vista has integrated thousands
of non-technical businesses without ever selling to one directly, and the workflow
library covers every routine job in a small-business office.

This is where the automation market actually is: tens of millions of businesses,
a meaningful fraction of their back-office payroll, and no incumbent who has ever
reached them. Even a small slice of that spend across a hundred thousand
businesses is a multi-billion-dollar revenue base.

Shape: $1B is not the ceiling. It is the point where business three is clearly
working.

### Why the sequence matters

Anyone can describe business three. The reason nobody has built it is that the
first customers are unreachable. Business one solves that by borrowing the PE
industry's acquisition machine. Business two turns each solved customer into a
recurring relationship and a data asset. Business three is what those assets are
worth once they compound. **The wedge is small on purpose. The market it opens is
not.**

## 4. Where this is in a few years

**Year one.** A handful of PE firms, a few dozen companies integrated, one category
where we have done it enough times that the blueprint is real. The recorder,
connectors, and agent runtime are in production. The first "company #20" deploys in
a fraction of the time "company #1" took, and we can show it.

**Year three.** Dozens of firms. Hundreds of companies running on Vista. Services
are a minority of revenue. A firm that signs a new platform deal puts Vista on the
integration checklist by default. The workflow library spans several categories,
and cross-category jobs like collections and accounts payable deploy from a shared
blueprint. We have the first companies that exited a rollup and stayed on Vista
under a new owner.

**Year five.** Vista is the operating layer for a meaningful share of
lower-middle-market private equity in the United States. Thousands of companies.
The first inbound customers who were never acquired by anyone, referred by someone
who watched it work. The workflow library is the most complete record of how
small-business back-office work is actually done that has ever existed, and it is
ours.

**Beyond.** The back office is no longer a department. It is a service, and Vista
is the one delivering it.

## The bet, in one paragraph

Back-office work in every small and mid-sized business is going to be automated.
The technology is arriving faster than the businesses can adopt it, and the gap is
distribution and trust, not capability. Private equity rollups are the one place
where a third party gets handed non-technical businesses with a mandate to change
how they run. We are the team that understands both the hesitant business and the
firm that bought it. We will use that position to integrate thousands of companies,
build the map of how their work gets done, and turn that map into the platform
every business runs its back office on. That is how a services wedge into a niche
becomes one of the largest software companies of the decade.
