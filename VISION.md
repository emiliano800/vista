## Story 1 — The back office that runs itself

It is 2031. A regional HVAC rollup owns fourteen branches across three states. Each
branch was once a family business with its own way of invoicing, its own
spreadsheet for scheduling, and one person who knew where everything was.

Today, none of those branches has a back office in the old sense. Work orders come
in and get scheduled. Parts get ordered when trucks run low. Invoices go out the day
a job closes, and follow-ups go out when they are late. Month-end close takes an
afternoon, and the CFO spends it reviewing exceptions, not building spreadsheets.
The general ledger reconciles itself against the bank feed every night.

The people who used to do that work are still there. They handle the customers who
call angry, the vendor who shipped the wrong compressor, the job that needs a
judgment call. The agents do the rest and hand every exception to a human with a
full explanation of what they saw and why they stopped.

Nobody at the rollup set any of this up. The PE firm that assembled the company
handed each branch to Vista on the day the deal closed. Vista connected the
systems, watched how the work was actually done, built the agents, and stayed
accountable for the results. That is the whole product: **back-office work,
delivered as an outcome, to businesses that would never have built it themselves.**

Every category of routine office work in every small and mid-sized business is the
addressable market. Rollups are how we get there first.

## Story 2 — Company #101

The first HVAC company Vista integrated took a forward-deployed engineer four
months. The tenth took six weeks. The fortieth took eight days, and most of that
was waiting on system access.

What changed was not the engineer. It was the library.

Every deployment leaves behind system mappings, recorded workflow demonstrations,
exception-handling rules, agent trajectories, and the human corrections that
taught the agents where they were wrong. By the fortieth HVAC company, Vista
already knew that dispatchers batch-schedule on Thursday afternoons, that
warranty callbacks get coded three different ways, and that the QuickBooks
"Uncategorized" account is where the interesting problems hide.

Company #101 in a category starts from a near-complete blueprint. The FDE confirms
the blueprint fits, adjusts the ten percent that is genuinely different, and turns
it on. What was a services engagement is now a deployment. What was a deployment
becomes a checkbox.

Then the library crosses categories. Accounts payable in a dental rollup looks a
lot like accounts payable in a landscaping rollup. Collections is collections. The
workflow library stops being organized by industry and starts being organized by
job. At that point Vista is no longer an integration firm with good tooling. It is
a **compounding, proprietary map of how small-business work actually gets done**,
and nobody else has one, because nobody else was in the room for a thousand
deployments.

## Story 3 — The default operating layer for private equity

Lower-middle-market private equity owns tens of thousands of companies at any
given time, and buys thousands more every year. Each acquisition triggers the same
sequence: figure out the systems, figure out the people, figure out the numbers,
fold it into the platform, prove the value-creation thesis to the investment
committee.

In this story, that sequence has a default answer. A firm signs a platform deal,
and Vista is on the integration checklist next to the lawyers and the lenders.
Acquisition day is Vista day. The firm's operating partner does not ask "how are we
going to integrate this one?" because the answer has been the same for the last
nine.

The economics that make this stick:

- The firm's integration cost per add-on falls every year while its deal pace goes up.
- The reporting the LPs see is generated from connected systems, not collected by
  analysts over email.
- The value-creation initiatives the firm promised in its investment memo are
  tracked from proposed to realized, with evidence, inside the same platform.

Vista becomes the layer that every portfolio company in the fund runs through,
and the platform the next fund is raised on. When a firm shows LPs that its
portfolio's SG&A ratio is four points better than peers because of how it
integrates, that is a fundraising argument, and Vista is the reason.

The ambition is not a big customer list. It is that **"we run on Vista" is a line
in the diligence deck**, the way "we use Salesforce" stopped being a differentiator
and became a baseline.

## Story 4 — From rollups to every hesitant business

There are roughly thirty million small businesses in the United States. Most of
them want what AI is supposed to do for them and have no idea how to get it. They
have been sold chatbots and dashboards. They have signed up for tools, not
configured them, and cancelled. They do not have an IT person. They are not going
to become one.

Every pure workflow-automation company has run into this wall. The technology
works; the customer cannot be reached, cannot be onboarded, and cannot be kept.

Vista's path around the wall is that it never sells to these businesses cold. A PE
firm acquires them and Vista arrives with the deal. The business does not evaluate
a vendor; it gets handed an operating team. By the time the rollup is sold, the
business has been running on Vista agents for three years and cannot imagine going
back.

Then the rollup exits. The new owner, a strategic buyer or another fund, inherits
a company that runs on Vista. The former owners start new businesses and want the
same thing. The branch manager who watched it work leaves to run a competitor and
calls Vista first. The accountant who serviced the rollup starts recommending it to
every client with a scheduling problem.

The PE wedge is how Vista gets its first several thousand non-technical businesses
without ever running a self-serve funnel. Those businesses are the seed of a
distribution channel that the pure-play automation companies would have paid
anything to build. **Vista uses one industry's acquisition machine to bootstrap
distribution into the largest and hardest-to-reach market in the economy.**

## Story 5 — The Vista premium

The last story is about how the economics close.

A company that runs on Vista has connected systems, documented workflows, agents
handling routine work, a clean audit trail, and financials that reconcile to
source. A buyer doing diligence on it finds no black boxes. The integration risk
that normally discounts a small acquisition is gone because the integration is
already done.

Those companies trade at a premium. Not a huge one at first. A half-turn of EBITDA,
then a full turn, as buyers learn that "Vista-integrated" means the back office is
a known quantity. Firms start acquiring companies specifically to put them on
Vista, because the arbitrage between an unintegrated business and an integrated one
is now measurable.

At that point Vista is not a cost line in the integration budget. It is part of
the return. The firm's model has a Vista line in the value-creation bridge next to
pricing and procurement. Vista's pricing follows: a share of the measured, realized
savings, not a per-seat fee. The incentives of the firm, the portfolio company, and
Vista are the same, which is the only arrangement that has ever worked in this
market.

The end state: **Vista is how private capital turns operationally messy businesses
into clean ones**, and the market prices that transformation. Everything upstream,
the recorder, the workflow library, the agent runtime, the FDE, exists to make
that transformation cheaper and more certain each time.

## What all five stories have in common

- The customer buys an outcome. Nobody configures anything.
- Every deployment makes the next one cheaper. If a feature does not do that, it
  is not on the roadmap.
- The PE firm is the wedge, not the destination. The destination is every business
  that has a back office and no way to automate it.
- Trust is the product. Every number traces to a source. Every agent action has an
  explanation. Every exception goes to a person.
